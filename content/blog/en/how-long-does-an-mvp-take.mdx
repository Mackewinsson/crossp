Every founder asks this in roughly the same tone: half hopeful, half bracing for bad news. "How long does an MVP take?" Usually there's a fundraising deadline behind it, or a sales demo already booked, or a board meeting where someone will ask "so, where's the product?" The honest answer is unsatisfying: it depends entirely on what you're calling an MVP. And most teams, without meaning to, inflate that label until it quietly means "the whole product, but rushed."

We've sat in enough of these conversations to notice the pattern. Someone starts with "just the basics" and by the third planning call, "the basics" includes a referral system, three user roles, and an admin dashboard with export-to-PDF. None of that is wrong to want eventually. It's wrong to call it an MVP.

## What an MVP actually is

Strip away the buzzword and an MVP is just the **smallest production-grade slice** that lets one real user complete one valuable job, start to finish, without someone from your team quietly fixing things behind the scenes.

It is not:

- a clickable prototype with no real backend behind it,
- a full multi-role platform justified by "we'll need it eventually,"
- or a feature checklist copied from whoever you consider your biggest competitor.

Here's a test we use with clients: if your "MVP" spec covers every edge case, every admin report, and every third-party integration you can imagine needing, you're not scoping an MVP. You're scoping v2 and calling it v1 out of habit.

## Timeline bands that tend to hold up

We'll give you bands, not a promise — because a promise from a stranger on the internet is worth exactly nothing.

- **2–4 weeks.** This is a genuinely narrow slice: auth, one core flow, a deploy pipeline. It only happens this fast when scope was already clear going in and integrations are light or nonexistent.
- **4–8 weeks.** The more typical focused MVP — core workflow plus admin basics, a staging environment, and something that's actually production-ready rather than a demo that breaks if you click the wrong thing.
- **8–12+ weeks.** Payments, several user roles with different permissions, AI features that need real evaluation, or legacy integrations that nobody fully documented — any of these push you here, sometimes past it.

A quick pattern worth knowing: anything advertised as "done in 10 days" while promising full production quality is usually skipping something. Often it's tests. Sometimes it's the handover documentation. Occasionally it's both, and you find out three months later when the original developer is unreachable.

### Why timelines slip more than teams admit

It's rarely the coding that's slow. It's usually decision latency on the buyer's side — waiting three days for a Slack reply about button copy, or a stakeholder who wants to see "one more option" before approving a flow that was already agreed on. We don't say this to blame anyone; fast decisions are genuinely hard when you're juggling five other things. But it's worth naming, because it's the schedule killer nobody puts in the Gantt chart.

## What "done" should actually mean

Whatever timeline you land on, agree on this in writing before the clock starts — not after week six, when it becomes a negotiation instead of a checklist:

- Users can complete the primary workflow end to end, not just click through a happy path with fake data.
- Code lives in **your** repository. Credentials and cloud accounts are under **your** accounts, not a personal account someone will "transfer later."
- A staging environment exists, and the production deploy process is documented well enough that someone other than the original builder could run it.
- Critical paths have automated tests, or at minimum a QA checklist specific enough that "it works" means something.
- You can watch weekly demos of working software — not just receive status update emails that describe progress you can't verify.

> A launch date without a written definition of "done" is just a rumor with a calendar invite attached.

## How to compress the timeline without wrecking quality

If the deadline is real and non-negotiable — fundraising rounds don't wait for anyone — here's where the actual leverage is:

- Cut scope before you cut quality. Fewer features done properly beats more features held together with duct tape.
- Sequence deliberately: core workflow first, admin essentials second, secondary integrations last. Nobody's first real user needs the CSV export.
- Prefer weekly shippable increments over one big-bang reveal at the end. It's less dramatic, but it's how you catch a wrong assumption in week two instead of week nine.
- Make decisions fast on your end. Seriously — this one is mostly in your control, and it moves the needle more than almost any technical choice.

We've watched teams shave two or three weeks off a schedule just by getting faster at saying yes or no to a mockup, without touching the actual build.

## A timeline is a scope decision wearing a calendar

Here's the thing worth internalizing: when someone asks "can you do this in four weeks?", they're really asking two questions at once, and only one of them is about speed. The other is "what are you willing to cut to hit that date?" A partner who says yes to any timeline without renegotiating scope is either about to disappoint you, or about to quietly skip the parts you won't notice until they matter — the tests, the documentation, the edge cases nobody demoed.

The better version of that conversation sounds like: "Four weeks gets you the core flow and basic admin. Payments and the second role wait for sprint two." That's not a worse answer. It's a more honest one, and it's the answer that actually protects your launch date instead of just naming one.
